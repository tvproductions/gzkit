## The text

### Centralized control and decentralized execution (Chapter I § 3, pages I-3 and I-4)

> b. Joint air operations are normally conducted using centralized control and decentralized execution to achieve effective control and foster initiative, responsiveness, and flexibility. Centralized control is giving one commander the responsibility and authority for planning, directing, and coordinating a military operation or group/category of operations.

> Decentralized execution is the delegation of execution authority to subordinate commanders. Decentralized execution is essential to generate the required tempo of operations and to cope with the uncertainty, disorder, and fluidity of combat in air operations.

> (1) Mission command is the conduct of military operations through decentralized execution based upon mission-type orders and is a key component of the C2 function. Commanders delegate decisions to subordinates wherever possible, which minimizes detailed control and empowers subordinates’ initiative to make decisions based on the commander’s guidance rather than constant communications.

> (3) Over-controlling joint air operations can reduce tactical flexibility, taking away initiative from operators. Insufficient guidance may result in failure to capitalize on joint force integration or may degrade operational-level flexibility, such as shifting JFC priorities, thus reducing effectiveness.

> (4) Recent advances in technology and communication have provided commanders with a much greater ability to exercise highly centralized control and even, potentially, centralized execution. Commanders should be cautious in exercising such capability except for very limited and highly sensitive missions. No single commander or headquarters can have the necessary situational awareness or maintain the tempo of operations required to effectively execute tactical operations in a highly dynamic combat environment.

How much of each is a matter of the mission (§ 3b(2)): missions with "a high degree of uncertainty and complexity" "require greater latitude in mission tasking and a higher degree of decentralized execution", while "highly sensitive strike missions against long-range strategic targets will generally require a higher level of detailed planning and more centralized control."

Where standing guidance travels (§ 3c):

> This includes adherence to the JFC’s approved guidance provided by the ROE, airspace control plan (ACP), airspace control order (ACO), area air defense plan (AADP), and special instructions (SPINS) located in the air tasking order (ATO).

### The joint air tasking cycle (Chapter III § 6, pages III-19 to III-27)

> a. The joint air tasking cycle provides for the effective and efficient employment of joint air capabilities and forces made available. This process provides an iterative, cyclic process for the planning, apportionment, allocation, coordination, and tasking of joint air missions and sorties within the guidance of the JFC.

> A timely ATO is critical—other joint force components conduct their planning and operations based on a prompt, executable ATO and are dependent on its information.

> b. The joint air tasking cycle begins with the JFC’s objectives, incorporates guidance received during JFC and component coordination, and culminates with assessment of previous actions. The ATO articulates the tasking for joint air operations for a specific execution timeframe, normally 24 hours. The joint air tasking cycle is synchronized with the JFC’s battle rhythm. The JAOC normally establishes a 72- to 96-hour ATO planning cycle. The battle rhythm or daily operations cycle (schedule of events) articulates briefings, meetings, and report requirements.

> The net result of the tasking process is a series of ATOs and related products in various stages of progress at any time (see Figure III-12). The primary factor that drives the daily schedule for the development of the ATO is the battle rhythm. The battle rhythm is a detailed timeline that lists a series of briefings, meetings, etc., to produce specific products by a specified time to support decision making.

> e. Joint Air Tasking Cycle Stages. The joint air tasking cycle consists of six stages (see Figure III-13).

> Unlike the joint targeting cycle, the joint air tasking cycle is time-dependent, built around finite time periods to plan, prepare for, and conduct joint air operations.

The six stages, by their headings:

1. Objectives, Effects, and Guidance
2. Target Development
3. Weaponeering and Allocation
4. ATO Production and Dissemination
5. Execution Planning and Force Execution
6. Assessment

Figure III-13 names the products of each: the air operations directive from stage 1; the joint integrated prioritized target list from stage 2; the master air attack plan and the sortie allotment message from stage 3; the ATO, ACO and SPINS from stage 4; and from execution, "Physical damage assessment; inflight reports, mission reports". Figure III-12 shows five orders, A to E, each moving through directive, target list, send, fly and assess, staggered a day apart.

From stage 2:

> The product of this effort, when approved by the JFC or the JFC’s designated representative (e.g., JTCB), is the JIPTL.

From stage 3:

> All approved targets are weaponeered, to include recommended aimpoints, weapon systems and munitions, fusing, target identification and description, desired direct effects of target attack, probability of creating the desired effect, and collateral damage concerns.

> The resulting MAAP is the plan for employment that forms the foundation of the ATO.

From stage 4:

> The ATO production team constructs, publishes, and disseminates the daily ATO and applicable SPINS to appropriate forces.

> Instructions are contained in SPINS and in the ACO and are updated as frequently as required. The AOD, ATO, ACO, and SPINS provide operational and tactical direction at appropriate levels of detail. The level of detail should be very explicit when forces operate from different bases and multi-component or composite missions are tasked. In contrast, less detail is required when missions are tasked to a single component or base.

From stage 5:

> (a) The JAOC must be responsive to required changes during the execution of the ATO. In-flight reports, discovery of time-sensitive targets (TSTs), and an initial assessment (such as battle damage assessment [BDA]) may cause a redirecting of joint air capabilities and forces before launch or a redirection once airborne.

> (b) During execution, the JAOC is the focal point for changes to the ATO and is the centralized control node for tasking of joint air capabilities and forces.

> It is essential, however, that the JAOC be notified of all redirected missions.

From stage 6:

> (6) Stage 6: Assessment. Assessment is performed by all levels of the joint force.

> Air planners should determine MOPs to evaluate task accomplishment and MOEs to assess any changes in system behavior and/or capability or in the operational environment. Planners should ensure they establish logical links between air objectives and tasks and the measures used to evaluate them early in the planning process.

> This includes a determination and assessment of actual collateral damage.

> (e) Although assessment appears to mark the end of the air tasking cycle, it is an ongoing activity that provides important inputs to decision making and aiding processes throughout that cycle.

### The operations centre (Appendix E, page E-1)

> The JAOC director is charged with effectively managing joint air operations and establishing the JAOC battle rhythm.

> Despite physical proximity to the ATO planning, production, and execution areas within the JAOC, SD personnel should not become caught up in execution details.

The appendix divides the centre by time horizon: a strategy division for long-range and near-term planning; a combat plans division for the 48 hours before an order executes, which "develops the MAAP, assembles the SPINS"; and a combat operations division "responsible for the execution of the current ATO".

### Glossary

> **air tasking order.** A method used to task and disseminate to components, subordinate units, and command and control agencies projected sorties, capabilities, and/or forces to targets and specific missions. Also called ATO.

> **joint air operations center.** A jointly staffed facility established for planning, directing, and executing joint air operations in support of the joint force commander’s operation or campaign objectives. Also called JAOC.

> **sortie.** In air operations, an operational flight by one aircraft.

> **mission.** ... 3. The dispatching of one or more aircraft to accomplish one particular task.

> **centralized control.** ... 2. In joint air operations, placing within one commander the responsibility and authority for planning, directing, and coordinating a military operation or group/category of operations.

> **decentralized execution.** Delegation of execution authority to subordinate commanders.

The glossary has no separate entry for special instructions; the term is spelled out in the abbreviations list and used in the text.

## Notes for the record

- **A sortie is one flight by one aircraft; a mission is one or more aircraft sent to do one task.** The publication keeps the two apart.
- **The order is dated and short-lived**: one execution period, "normally 24 hours", with several orders in different stages at once.
- **Standing guidance rides inside the order** as special instructions; it is not carried separately by each crew.
- **Detail scales with coordination, not with rank**: "very explicit" when several bases or components fly together, "less detail" for a single one.
- **Control is centralized; execution is delegated**, and the publication warns against centralized execution even where technology allows it.
- **The centre is told of every redirection**, including those a delegated commander makes.
- **Assessment is the sixth stage and also continuous**, and it includes "actual collateral damage".
- **Battle rhythm is the publication's own term**: "a detailed timeline" of events that "produce specific products by a specified time to support decision making".
- **Planners keep out of execution**: the strategy division "should not become caught up in execution details".
