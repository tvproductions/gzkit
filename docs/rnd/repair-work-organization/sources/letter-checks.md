## Short findings

1. **No regulation read defines A, B, C or D checks.** The FAA speaks of them, in rulemaking preambles and airworthiness directives, as an industry term that varies by operator. Its firmest statement is that it will not write them into a mandate: "letter checks vary among different operators and can be escalated" (72 FR 46577).
2. **The FAA's nearest thing to a definition concerns "heavy maintenance check", a statutory term, not the letters.** "the FAA considers an HMV or HMC to consist of a ``C'' check or segment thereof, a ``D'' check or segment thereof, or other scheduled maintenance where structural inspections are accomplished" (70 FR 5521).
3. **No regulation read ties a check letter to a hangar.** EASA sorts maintenance into line and base by the work, the environment and the duration, and requires hangars for planned base maintenance. A letter does not place a check in either category.
4. **In the practice described by secondary sources, hangars are normal for C and D. A is contested. Transit and daily are ramp work. B has mostly ceased to exist.** So the texts do not support "all of them require a hangar".
5. **The letters belong to operators and legacy programs.** Under MSG-3 the MRB and MPD give tasks and intervals, and the operator packages them. Under the older MSG-2 practice the MRB document itself carried a C-check interval.

## How the FAA itself speaks of the checks

### Where the terms do not occur

Title 14 has no lettered check. The repository record found "check" within Part 121 Subpart L only as the ETOPS pre-departure service check, and no lettered check in the subpart. AC 120-16G and AC 121-22D use no letter-check term either. AC 121-22D gives "no name to a packaged visit".

The statute has the broader term only:

> Inspections and reviews required under subsection (a)(1) of this section shall be carried out as part of each heavy maintenance check of the aircraft conducted after the 14th year in which the aircraft has been in service.

[P] 49 U.S.C. § 44717. The regulation that implements the aging airplane inspection (§ 121.1105) sets intervals "not to exceed 7 years" and, in the passages read, uses no check name.

### The FAA's nearest definition

> To comply with the statute, the FAA considers an HMV or HMC to consist of a ``C'' check or segment thereof, a ``D'' check or segment thereof, or other scheduled maintenance where structural inspections are accomplished.

[P] FAA response, Aging Airplane Safety, 70 FR 5518, at 5521 (Feb. 2, 2005). AC 120-84 CHG 1 appears to repeat this ("A heavy maintenance check is a "C" check or segment thereof, a "D"...") **[snippet]**. faa.gov refused the document.

An earlier FAA definition of "heavy maintenance check" was proposed in 1993 and later withdrawn. Its wording was not found:

> Those proposals included: (1) A definition of the terms ``heavy maintenance check'' (HMC) and ``years in service'';

> Several commenters also were concerned that the definition of ``heavy maintenance check'' was too broad.

[P] Aging Airplane Safety, 64 FR 16298, at 16299 (Apr. 2, 1999), describing Notice 93-14, 58 FR 51944.

### The FAA surveyed the letters and found they do not hold still

> Among those considered were the heavy maintenance check interval, heavy maintenance visit interval, or the ``letter check'' (e.g., ``C'', ``D'', or ``E'') interval or other equivalent check interval an operator may use.

> The FAA also considered the phasing and segmenting of heavy maintenance checks and found that the intervals varied from 1 to 27 years.

[P] 64 FR at 16300. Note the "E": the FAA's own examples run past D. The 2002 final rule repeats the finding and states the result:

> The FAA also considered the phasing and segmenting of HMCs and found that the intervals varied from 1 to 27 years. Therefore, the FAA chose to establish a fixed repeat inspection interval.

[P] 67 FR 72726, at 72730 (Dec. 6, 2002). The same rule records the trade association's numbers, as a recommendation, not an FAA finding:

> ``a `C' check compliance period (18 months) or `D' check period (5 years) be adopted for all rules unless it can be shown that a shorter time interval is required for safety reasons.''

[P-commenter] ATA memorandum 96-AE-014, March 11, 1996, quoted at 67 FR 72730.

### The FAA refuses letters in mandates

> We cannot specify a letter check for mandatory inspection intervals because letter checks vary among different operators and can be escalated.

[P] Boeing 767 supplemental NPRM, 72 FR 46576, at 46577 (Aug. 21, 2007).

> Since maintenance schedules vary among operators, there would be no assurance that the airplane would be inspected during the maximum interval if we were to allow operators the option of inspecting at the next C-check.

[P] Boeing 757 final rule, 72 FR 60244, at 60245 (Oct. 24, 2007).

An exception exists in one M7 Aerospace AD, where the letter is one of several accepted events:

> At the next Zone related Phase or Letter Check inspection after June 15, 2016 (the effective date of this AD) or within the next 600 hours time-in-service after June 15, 2016 (the effective date of this AD), whichever occurs later;

[P] M7 Aerospace AD, 81 FR 29123 (May 11, 2016). A second clause, a 6-month limit, bounds it. The 737 AD at 67 FR 17917 also speaks of "maintenance events (e.g., letter checks)" in a reporting paragraph, and the FAA revised that paragraph to ask for "just estimated dates" instead.

### The FAA uses the letters as ordinary planning vocabulary

> It is desirable to incorporate these inspections in the current heavy maintenance program, such as a ``C'' or ``D'' check, without taking airplanes out of service.

[P] Fuel tank rule, 66 FR 23086, at 23097 (May 7, 2001). The aircraft is treated as out of service during such a check, since the inspection is put there to avoid a separate removal from service.

> We believe that any of these alterations would be scheduled to occur during a period of allocated downtime such as a scheduled maintenance ``C Check.''

> even inspections of EWIS located in the most severe environment with the highest risk of accidental damage may be performed at intervals ranging from an ``A'' check to a ``1-C'' check, which are normally scheduled maintenance intervals.

[P] EAPAS/FTS final rule, 72 FR 63364, at 63388 and 63389 (Nov. 8, 2007).

> the heavy maintenance inspection intervals are typically every 4 to 5 years.

[P] 66 FR at 23105. This is the FAA's general statement. It conflicts with Ackert's 6 to 12 years for the D-check below, and with 1 to 27 years above.

### Accident and audit reports

The NTSB report names A, B and C checks, not D. It gives the most exact primary account of one operator's program.

> The MRB report and the resulting OAMP document recommended C-check intervals of 3,500 flight hours or 15 months, whichever came first. Alaska Airlines' initial C-check interval was 2,500 flight hours. In 1988, it was extended to every 13 months (which, based on the average airplane utilization rate at Alaska Airlines at the time, was about 3,200 flight hours). In 1996, the C-check interval was extended to 15 months (which, based on the average airplane utilization rate at Alaska Airlines at the time, was about 4,775 flight hours).

> Walk-around check—accomplished by Alaska Airlines maintenance personnel after each arrival.

> Service check—accomplished when the airplane remains on the ground for 8 or more hours at a maintenance facility capable of conducting at least A check maintenance.

> A check—accomplished at intervals not to exceed 250 flight hours.

[P] NTSB/AAR-02/01, § 1.6.3.1.2, p. 26. Alaska's General Maintenance Manual of February 5, 1998, as reported by the Board.

> In July 1988, B checks were eliminated from Alaska Airlines' MD-80 maintenance program, and the tasks accomplished during B checks were incorporated into the A and C checks. A checks were to be accomplished every 125 flight hours.

[P] NTSB/AAR-02/01, § 1.6.3.2.1.2, p. 32. The same section records B-check intervals of 350 and then 500 flight hours.

> On May 27, 1993, a C check was accomplished at the OAK maintenance facility.

[P] NTSB/AAR-02/01, § 1.6.3.4, p. 49.

The inspector general's reports do **not** name lettered checks. A search of AV-2008-090 and AV-2013-073 finds no "A check", "C check" or "D check" in either spelling. They speak of heavy checks:

> The nine major air carriers we reviewed [footnote marker omitted] sent 71 percent of their heavy airframe maintenance checks to repair stations in 2007, up from only 34 percent in 2003 (see figure 1). This work includes performing complete teardowns of aircraft that can take up to 7 weeks to complete.

[P] AV-2008-090, Results in Brief.

> FAA defines substantial maintenance as major airframe maintenance checks; significant engine work (e.g., complete teardown/overhaul); major alterations or major repairs performed on airframes, engines, or propellers; repairs made to emergency equipment; and/or aircraft painting.

[P] AV-2008-090, footnote 5.

> ... to heavy airframe checks, which involve a complete teardown and overhaul of the aircraft.

[P] AV-2013-073.

## The chain from regulation to a lettered check

| Link | Who | Instrument |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Must have a program | FAA, by rule | § 121.367, § 121.369 |
| 2. Time limits are authorized | FAA, by operations specifications | § 119.49(a)(8) |
| 3. Tasks and intervals are set | Manufacturer, industry and FAA, through MRB | MRBR, AC 121-22D |
| 4. Tasks and intervals are published for planners | Manufacturer | MPD (OAMP for the MD-80) |
| 5. Operator builds its program | Operator, for regulator approval | CAMP |
| 6. Tasks are packaged into visits, lettered or not | Operator | Scheduled work packages |

**Link 1, the regulation requires the program.**

> Each certificate holder shall have an inspection program and a program covering other maintenance, preventive maintenance, and alterations that ensures that—

[P] 14 CFR § 121.367. Continuing with (b):

> Competent personnel and adequate facilities and equipment are provided for the proper performance of maintenance, preventive maintenance, and alterations; and

[P] § 121.367(b). "Adequate facilities" is the whole of the rule's statement in this section. It names no hangar.

> The certificate holder's manual must contain the programs required by § 121.367

[P] § 121.369(b).

**Link 2, time limits sit in operations specifications.**

> Time limitations, or standards for determining time limitations, for overhauling, inspecting, and checking airframes, engines, propellers, rotors, appliances, and emergency equipment.

[P] § 119.49(a)(8).

> The FAA authorizes your maintenance schedule through your operations specifications (OpSpecs), and your Continuing Analysis and Surveillance System (CASS) monitors that schedule to verify its effectiveness

[P] AC 120-16G, § 6-2.

**Link 3, the MRB process sets minimum tasks and intervals.**

> This AC refers to these minimum scheduled maintenance tasking/interval requirements as the Maintenance Review Board Report (MRBR), the Maintenance Type Board Report (MTBR), or the Original Equipment Manufacturer (OEM)/Type Certificate Holder (TCH) Recommended Maintenance Procedures. After FAA approval, the requirements become a basis upon which operators develop their own individual maintenance programs.

[P] AC 121-22D, § 1.1.

> The resulting report (MRBR or MTBR) is produced and owned by the OEM/TCH, accepted by the Industry Steering Committee (ISC), and approved by the FAA.

[P] AC 121-22D, § 8.1.

**Link 4, the manufacturer publishes planning documents.** The repository record held that the MPD's derivation from the MRB report was industry description only. A primary text now states it, for the MD-80's equivalent document:

> On the basis of the guidance in the applicable MRB report, manufacturers issue on-aircraft maintenance planning (OAMP) documents and generic task cards outlining specific maintenance tasks. The tasks and intervals in an OAMP document are typically the same as those contained in the associated MRB report.

[P] NTSB/AAR-02/01, § 1.6.3.1.1, p. 25.

> Task intervals are specified in terms of a frequency and usage parameter such as flight hours, cycles, and calendar time. Letter checks are not used.

[S] Ackert, describing the MPD.

**Link 5, the operator builds the program.**

> Operators will use an MRBR, MTBR, or OEM/TCH requirements document as a basis for developing and designing a maintenance program and submit it to the local regulatory authority for approval.

[P] AC 121-22D, § 8.2.

> After approval by the FAA, the requirements become a base or framework around which each air carrier develops its own individual maintenance program.

[P] AC 121-22A, as quoted by the NTSB in AAR-02/01, footnote 52.

**Link 6, the operator packages the tasks.** This is the link that carries the letters.

> The regulations are broad enough to permit you to organize all of these individual tasks into a series of integrated scheduled work packages of your own design that provide a continuous succession of necessary or desirable scheduled maintenance tasks for your entire airplane.

[P] AC 120-16G, § 6-1. This is the FAA's own statement of the freedom. It does not use a letter.

> Contemporary practice removes the packaging of maintenance tasks from the MRB process. When a MSG analysis is conducted only the tasks and intervals are identified. The packaging into manageable blocks is left to the operator of the airplane.

[S] Hessburg, 2000. He is identified in the article as recently retired from Boeing, where he was Chief Mechanic.

> In the airline industry, a letter check is the alphabetic designation given to scheduledmaintenance maintenance packages.

[S] Ackert, § 4.1. [sic]

> For modern aircraft types (e.g. B737NG family and B777), the 'letter check' distinctions are often less important, since MSG-3 task-orientated maintenance programs are employed.

[S] Ackert, § 4.1.

> While original equipment manufacturers (OEMs), including Airbus, now rarely refer to A and C checks within the MPDs, these terms are often used by airline engineers and maintenance, repair & overhaul (MRO) providers to refer to light and heavier airframe checks.

[S] *Aircraft Commerce* no. 111, 2017.

**One qualification to link 6.** Packaging was not always the operator's alone. Three sources show that legacy MRB documents carried letter intervals:

- The NTSB passage above: "The MRB report and the resulting OAMP document recommended C-check intervals" [P].
- "the historical set-up of tasks with the same interval being grouped into a letter check in the maintenance review board (MRB) of legacy aircraft types" [S] *Aircraft Commerce* no. 106.
- "Maintenance Review Board (MRB) heavy maintenance visit (4C), which is scheduled at a 72-month interval" [P-commenter] 70 FR 5366 (Feb. 2, 2005), on the Boeing 757.

Hessburg's "Contemporary practice removes the packaging" implies the same change. The texts read do not date it.

The 787 is the far end of that line:

> "Rather than letter checks, we group the 787's maintenance tasks into phase checks (line checks) and base (hangar) checks,"

[S] Lee Burgess, Monarch Aircraft Engineering, quoted in *Aircraft Commerce* no. 106, 2016. Note his sorting by "line" and "base (hangar)", which replaces the letters.

## Line and base maintenance: the regulatory text

### EASA

> 'Line maintenance' refers to limited maintenance for the aircraft suitable to be carried out whilst the aircraft remains in the air operation environment.

> Line maintenance may include:

> trouble shooting;
> defect rectification;
> component replacement with use of external test equipment if required. Component replacement may include components such as engines and propellers;
> maintenance that will detect obvious unsatisfactory conditions/discrepancies/malfunctions, but does not require extensive in-depth inspection. It may also include internal structure, systems and powerplant items which are visible through quick opening access panels/doors/ports;
> repairs, modifications and other maintenance tasks which do not require extensive disassembly and can be accomplished by simple means.

> 'Base maintenance' refers to any maintenance for the aircraft other than line maintenance.

[P] AMC1 145.A.10, points (a) and (b), ED Decision 2022/011/R.

The sorting is the organisation's own, by procedure:

> Organisations maintaining aircraft should have a procedure to determine whether the tasks or groups of tasks to be carried out fall under the line maintenance or base maintenance scope of the organisation, with due regard to the expected duration of the maintenance, number and type of tasks, shifts and disciplines involved, work environment, etc.

> For temporary or occasional cases, the organisation may also have a procedure which allows, subject to a task assessment (including all relevant aspects and conditions), to conduct a base maintenance task under line maintenance environment.

> In particular, maintenance tasks of aircraft subject to 'progressive' or 'equalised' maintenance programmes should be individually assessed in respect of such procedure to ensure that all the tasks within the particular check can be carried out safely and to the required standards at the designated line maintenance station.

[P] AMC1 145.A.10, points (c) and (d). Point (d) is the closest regulatory text to a scheduled "check" at a line station. It contemplates that one can be there.

The hangar requirement attaches to base maintenance:

> For base maintenance of aircraft, aircraft hangars are both available and large enough to accommodate aircraft on planned base maintenance;

[P] 145.A.25(a)(1), Regulation (EU) No 1321/2014.

> refers to a closed facility that can house an aircraft and protect it from environmental conditions.

[P] GM1 to Annex II (Part-145), Definitions, "Base maintenance hangar".

> For line maintenance of aircraft, hangars are not essential but it is recommended that access to hangar accommodation be demonstrated for usage during inclement weather for minor scheduled work and lengthy defect rectification.

[P] AMC1 145.A.25(a), point 3.

And base maintenance is not strictly bound to a hangar either:

> Subject to a risk assessment and agreement by the competent authority, the organisation may use facilities at the approved location other than a base maintenance hangar for certain aircraft base maintenance tasks, provided that those facilities offer levels of weather and environmental protection that are equivalent to those of a base maintenance hangar, as well as a suitable working environment for the particular work package. This does not exempt an organisation from the requirement to have a base maintenance hangar in order to be approved to conduct base maintenance at a given location.

[P] AMC1 145.A.25(a), point 4.

145.A.30(h) confirms that "check" is the regulation's word for a base visit, without a letter: "during the particular base maintenance check or work package" [P].

### FAA

> (d) Line maintenance means—
> (1) Any unscheduled maintenance resulting from unforeseen events; or
> (2) Scheduled checks that contain servicing and/or inspections that do not require specialized training, equipment, or facilities.

[P] 14 CFR § 145.3(d). This is the only FAA definition of line maintenance found. Its test is the same in kind as EASA's, a test of what the work needs. No FAA definition of "base maintenance" was found, and the FAA text read uses "heavy maintenance" instead.

> Each certificated repair station must provide—
> (1) Housing for the facilities, equipment, materials, and personnel consistent with its ratings and limitations.

> A certificated repair station may perform maintenance, preventive maintenance, or alterations on articles outside of its housing if it provides suitable facilities that are acceptable to the FAA and meet the requirements of § 145.103(a) so that the work can be done in accordance with the requirements of part 43 of this chapter.

[P] 14 CFR § 145.103(a), (b). The rule says "housing". It does not say "hangar". The word's absence from this section is the observation, and it applies to this section only.

The Congressional Research Service, a government author but not a regulator, gives the working US usage:

> Line Maintenance. Light, regular maintenance checks carried out to ensure that an aircraft is fit for flight. Line maintenance includes troubleshooting, defect rectification, and overnight maintenance.

> Airframe Heavy Maintenance. A detailed inspection of the airframe and certain components, including any applicable corrosion prevention programs and comprehensive structural inspection and overhaul of the aircraft. Heavy maintenance is comparatively labor-intensive.

[S] CRS R42876, 2012.

## Each check, from the text

### Pre-flight, walk-around, transit

> It has a pre-flight check performed before the first flight of each day, a transit check prior to all subsequent flights during the day and a daily check performed every 24 hours.

[S] *Aircraft Commerce* no. 40, of the 737-300/-400/-500.

> "Pre-flight and transit checks consume a similar number of MH, which total about 2.0,"

[S] Same source, quoting an operator.

**Disagreement:** on whether the walk-around is maintenance at all. Hessburg writes "Walk around inspection by flight crew is not normally a part of a maintenance program." [S]. Alaska's manual has a walk-around "accomplished by Alaska Airlines maintenance personnel after each arrival." [P]. Leeham's model airline has "a minimum of tasks performed by our flight crews before each flight, essentially a walk-around" [S].

### Daily, service, overnight

> This check travels under several common names and post-flight, maintenance pre-flight, service check, and overnight to name a few. It is the lowest scheduled check.

> A daily check is a cursory inspection of the aircraft to look for obvious damage and deterioration.

> The accomplishment of the daily check requires little in the way of specific equipment, tools, or facilities.

> Usually, a daily check is accomplished every 24 to 60 hours of accumulated flight time.

[S] Hessburg.

> Most 737s are operated on short-haul operations during the day and so daily checks are performed overnight in most cases.

[S] *Aircraft Commerce* no. 40. The same article gives 2.0 to 3.0 man-hours from one source and "up to an average of 13.5MH" from another. Durations in elapsed hours were not stated.

The FAA has used "daily check" in an AD for a specific fuel shutoff valve task, but in that AD it is a flightcrew or maintenance operational check, not a program level (80 FR 55527).

### A check

> This is the next higher level of scheduled maintenance. It is normally accomplished at a designated maintenance station in the route structure and includes the opening of access panels to check and service certain items. Some limited special tooling, servicing, and test equipment is required.

[S] Hessburg.

> The A-Check generally consists of a general inspection of the interior/exterior of the airplane with selected areas opened. The A-check is typically performed biweekly to monthly.

[S] Ackert.

> "These light checks typically comprise additional items too, such as deferred defects, nonairworthy logbook items, and heavier cleaning that cannot be done on a day-today basis. It is usually performed by line maintenance people overnight,"

> Begley estimates that an average A check will take 150MH to perform.

[S] *Aircraft Commerce* no. 111, quoting Brendan Begley, Dublin Aerospace, on the A320.

Stated intervals: "basic A check interval of 250FH" [S, 737-300/-400/-500]; "750FH, 750 flight cycles (FC), or 4 months (MO); whichever interval is reached first" [S, A320, 2017]; "1,000FH" for 787 MPD light checks [S]. Alaska's history runs 125, then 150, then 250 flight hours [P]. In operator terms: "The average A check was 55 days" [S, *Aircraft Commerce* no. 111].

**Disagreement, where.** Four sources differ:

- "designated maintenance station in the route structure" [S, Hessburg]
- "line maintenance people overnight" [S, *Aircraft Commerce*]
- "shorter (typically overnight) hangar event (often called A checks)" [S, Leeham, 2024]
- "usually performed in an airport hangar" [T, Wikipedia]

Under AMC1 145.A.25(a)(3), line maintenance may use a hangar and need not. So a hangar A check and a line A check are compatible, and the difference may be only that of operator practice. No regulation read decides it.

**Disagreement, effort.** The same Leeham author gives "about 80 work-hours" (part 8) and "About two man-weeks" (part 7) for the same notional A check. Those figures do not reconcile. *Aircraft Commerce* gives 150 MH. Wikipedia gives 50 to 70.

### B check

> This is a slightly more detailed check of components and systems. Special equipment and tests may be required. It does not involve, however, detailed disassembly or removal of components.

> Contemporary maintenance programs do not use the 'B' check interval. For a number of reasons, the tasks formerly defined for this interval have, for many airplanes, been distributed between the 'A' and 'C' check.

[S] Hessburg.

Ackert confirms by omission: "The three most commonly used letter checks consists of: 1.) A-Check, 2.) C-Check, and 3.) D-Check." The NTSB gives one dated instance of the elimination, Alaska in July 1988 [P], at 350 and 500 flight hours beforehand. Leeham 2024 confirms for the A320 MPD revision 45: "The B check was folded into the A check" [S]. Where it was performed is stated only by Wikipedia: "within 1 to 3 days in an airport hangar" [T].

### C check

> They are normally accomplished at the main maintenance base of the airline where specialized manpower, materials, tooling, and hangar facilities are available.

> This is an extensive check of individual systems and components for serviceability and function. It requires a thorough visual inspection of specified areas, components and systems as well as operational or functional checks. It is a high-level check that involves extensive tooling, test equipment, and special skill levels. 'C' checks remove the airplane from the revenue schedule for 3 to 5 days.

[S] Hessburg, 2000. "They" is the "heavy checks", C and D.

> The C-Check is typically scheduled every 12- 20 months depending on the operator, airplane type and utilization.

[S] Ackert, 2010.

Operators' statements in FAA dockets:

> UPS stated that the line maintenance environment is not conducive to this type of work due to the required ground time, labor, and other resources, which are available at its major maintenance facility. UPS stated that its current maintenance program is based on a 30-month C-check interval.

[P-commenter] Airbus AD, 77 FR 47273, at 47274 (Aug. 8, 2012).

> FedEx states that its A300 maintenance program currently requires heavy maintenance (C-check) to be performed at the earlier of every 3,500 flight hours or 30 months.

[P-commenter] 71 FR 62888, at 62889 (Oct. 27, 2006). The commenter also refers to component replacement "that cannot be performed at most line maintenance stations because of test equipment requirements".

Stated intervals: 4,000 FH [S, 737-300/-400/-500]; "7,500FH, 5,000FC or 24MO; whichever is reached first" [S, A320, 2017]; "12,000FH, 6,000FC and 3YE" base checks [S, 787]; 15 months, about 4,775 FH [P, Alaska MD-80, 1996]; 30 months [P-commenter, UPS, FedEx]; 18 months [P-commenter, ATA, 1996].

**Disagreement, duration.** 3 to 5 days [S, Hessburg, 2000]. "1 to 4 weeks" [T, Wikipedia]. Fourteen days as a planning unit: a commenter speaks of programs that "do not provide for 14-day downtimes" [P-commenter, 70 FR 5521]. "adding 2-3 days of ground time" for an extended light C-check is an increment, not a total [P-commenter, FR Doc. 2011-5156, Mar. 8, 2011; API excerpt only, document not read]. The difference may be fleet size, era, or light against heavy C checks: Ackert's tables separate "C-Light" and "C-Heavy". The sources do not reconcile it.

**Disagreement, classification.** The FAA counts a C check as a heavy maintenance check when structural inspections are at issue (70 FR 5521). Trade usage calls it "base". Neither the FAA nor EASA text read states that a C check is base maintenance. The EASA text would decide that task by task.

**Hangar, as a primary fact.** One NTSB passage places C-check work physically in a hangar: "accomplished during a C check, the task is accomplished by a mechanic standing on a tail-stand platform in the hangar" [P, p. 30-31]. The same report records lubrication at a line station "outside of the hangar (even if it was raining)", between C checks [P, p. 31]. That is one operator and one task. The FAA also treats hangar space as the ordinary constraint on heavy work: "the hangar space dedicated for the incoming aircraft is not available because of additional work required on the aircraft currently in the hangar" [P, 67 FR 72726].

### D check

> This can also be referred to as the Structural check. It includes detailed visual and other non-destructive test inspections of the aircraft structure. It is an intense inspection of the structure for evidence of corrosion, structural deformation, cracking, and other signs of deterioration or distress and involves extensive disassembly to gain access for inspection.

> This check removes the airplane from service for 20 or more days.

[S] Hessburg.

> The D-Check, or Heavy Maintenance Visit (HMV), occurs every 6-12 years, depending on the airplane type and utilization. Usually the aircraft is taken out of service for several weeks.

[S] Ackert.

**Disagreement, interval.** 6 to 12 years [S, Ackert]. "`D' check period (5 years)" [P-commenter, ATA, 1996]. "typically every 4 to 5 years" for heavy maintenance inspections, not named as D [P, FAA, 66 FR 23105]. 1 to 27 years across phased and segmented heavy checks [P, FAA, 64 FR 16300].

**Disagreement, duration.** "20 or more days" [S]. "several weeks" [S]. "up to 7 weeks" for heavy airframe checks generally, not named D [P, OIG 2008]. "up to 50,000 man-hours, and six months to a year" [T, Wikipedia]. The Wikipedia figure is an outlier against every source above.

**Disagreement, identity.** Ackert equates D with Heavy Maintenance Visit. The FAA's HMC or HMV includes C, D or other structural visits. Ackert also records that the D check may not exist as a discrete visit:

> The phased check – sometimes referred to as equalized or segmented check - apportions tasks to smaller packages that may be accomplished more frequently than the packages in a block check

> An operator, for example, may phase or segment, portions of its heavy maintenance tasks equally over the appropriate number of C-Checks.

[S] Ackert, § 4.2. Hessburg gives the reason: "Their accomplishment can remove the airplane from service for several weeks. This is considered unacceptable" [S].

### How block checks nest

In the block scheme, each higher check includes those below it: "Each letter check generally incorporates all the work covered by preceding checks, plus the tasks assigned at that letter-check interval." [S, Ackert, § 4.2].

## Naval counterpart (brief)

> Chief of Naval Operations (CNO) scheduled maintenance availabilities greater than six months in duration are:

> Overhaul. An availability scheduled for accomplishment of industrial maintenance and modernization.

> CNO scheduled maintenance availabilities less than six months in duration. Short, labor intensive availabilities scheduled for accomplishment of industrial maintenance and modernization. Types of these availabilities include:

[P, but an undated and likely superseded copy] JFMM Vol. II, Ch. 3, § 3.2. The list includes Selected Restricted Availability, Docking Selected Restricted Availability, and Phased Maintenance Availability. So the Navy scheme names a docking variant and a phased variant in the title. It distinguishes by duration against six months.

Continuous Maintenance Availability (CMAV) was not read. The NAVSEA notes refused retrieval. [snippet, paraphrase] The search summary of those notes gave a notional two weeks every four months.

## Hangars, stated directly

- **Required by name of check:** none, in any regulatory text read.
- **Required by rule for a category:** planned base maintenance, EASA 145.A.25(a)(1), with the equivalent-facility allowance in AMC1 145.A.25(a) point 4. FAA § 145.103 requires "housing" and allows work outside it on terms.
- **Normally in a hangar in described practice:** C and D, per Hessburg and the operators' docket comments, which speak of a maintenance base or heavy environment. Only Hessburg and the NTSB's one mechanic use the word "hangar" of a C check.
- **Disputed:** A.
- **Not a hangar matter in any source:** pre-flight, transit, daily.
- **"Possibly all":** not supported by the texts read. Leeham's 2024 piece calls A an overnight hangar event, and Wikipedia places A and B in an airport hangar. Against them stand Hessburg, *Aircraft Commerce*, and EASA's "hangars are not essential" for line maintenance.
