## Vol. 20, Ch. 8, Sec. 1 (`20_008_001.pdf`, 8900.1 CHG 912, 6/17/25)

> C. Category of Maintenance. The following should be used by CHs when providing the general description of work required by 14 CFR §§ 121.369(a) and 135.427(a). The CH, not the FAA, is responsible for classifying their work into these categories.

> a) Heavy Maintenance. An example of heavy maintenance could be the inspection and repair of the aircraft airframe performed at specified time intervals. Guidelines from the aircraft manufacturer, the National Aviation Authority (NAA), the FAA, and the European Union Aviation Safety Agency (EASA), and further refined by the CH, determine the intervals. There are four levels of inspections, usually termed "A," "B," "C," and "D" checks. Line maintenance includes "A" and "B" checks while "C" and "D" checks are heavy maintenance.

> b) Line Maintenance. Line maintenance includes light, regular checks to ensure that the aircraft is safe for flight. Line maintenance also includes troubleshooting, defect rectification, and component replacement. Aviation Maintenance Technicians (AMT) (A&P mechanics) diagnose and correct issues on the aircraft and carry out these checks on either an ad hoc basis or at a scheduled interval. Line maintenance consists of three primary activity categories: transit checks, daily and weekly checks, and "A" and "B" checks.

> 2) Maintenance providers may perform maintenance in locations such as a hangar, a line maintenance environment, or in a shop.

## Vol. 20, Ch. 8, Sec. 4 (`020_008_004.pdf`)

> a) Aircraft Heavy Maintenance. An example of heavy maintenance could be the inspection and repair of the aircraft airframe, performed at specified time intervals. These scheduled inspections are typically based on a fixed number of flight hours. There are four levels of inspections, usually termed "A", "B", "C", and "D" checks. "A" and "B" checks are normally considered part of line maintenance. "C" and "D" checks are classified as heavy maintenance.

## Vol. 20, Ch. 8, Sec. 5 (`20_008_005.pdf`)

> NOTE: Heavy maintenance work is defined in 49 U.S.C. § 44733(j) and Volume 20, Chapter 8, Section 1 as a C-check, D-check, or equivalent

## Vol. 20, Ch. 5, Sec. 1 (`20_005_001.pdf`)

> A. Maintenance Schedule (MS). The MS is the compilation of every scheduled maintenance, inspection, and preventive maintenance task or function that applies to a particular aircraft by make/model/series (M/M/S). These scheduled maintenance tasks with their associated TLs are organized by the CAMP operator into a series of integrated scheduled work packages which provide a continuous succession of the regularly scheduled maintenance.

> B. Time Limitations (TL). TLs define the what, when, and how of the CAMP operator's scheduled maintenance requirements. The TL lists specific items, establishes specific intervals (expressed as hours of time in service, cycles, and calendar-time, or some other identifiable increment), and associates them with detailed maintenance or inspection tasks

## Vol. 20, Ch. 5, Sec. 2 (`20_005_002.pdf`)

> The regulations are broad enough to permit the CAMP operator to organize all of these individual tasks into a series of integrated scheduled work packages of its own design that provide a continuous succession of necessary or desirable scheduled maintenance tasks for the CAMP operator's aircraft.

## Vol. 20, Ch. 5, Sec. 3 (`20_005_003.pdf`)

> The CAMP operator's program should include provisions for coordinating with the responsible Flight Standards office when significant MS changes are being pursued. This could include optimization of entire "check packages" or substantial interval escalations.
